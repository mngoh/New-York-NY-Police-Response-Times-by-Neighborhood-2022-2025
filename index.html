Police response times by neighborhood, New York City

2022 to 2025, with January to June 2026 held out · 15,610,022 calls from the public, 2022 to June 2026 · Martin Ngoh

PlanResidents Count

For New York's most urgent 911 calls, police took less than a minute longer to arrive in lower-income, majority-Black and majority-Hispanic neighborhoods than in the highest-income and majority-White ones. For less urgent calls, the wait was 28% to 79% longer. Inside the same precinct, calls from those neighborhoods waited no longer.

The question
Do 911 calls wait longer for police to arrive in some New York neighborhoods than in others, at the same call priority?

Critical calls, the most urgent crimes in progress: the median wait from the call being entered to the first officer arriving was 5.0 minutes in the lowest-income fifth of neighborhoods and 4.1 in the highest-income fifth; 4.9 in majority-Black, 5.0 in majority-Hispanic and 4.2 in majority-White neighborhoods. These medians hold call type, hour of the week and how busy the precinct was fixed. The gaps, 0.6 to 0.8 minutes, are larger than chance would give but under the 1-minute bar set before the analysis, though the intervals for the lowest-income fifth and majority-Hispanic neighborhoods reach about 1.1 minutes. Serious calls show the same: gaps of about 0.9 minutes (14% to 16%), under the 20% bar.

Less urgent calls: for non-critical crimes in progress, such as harassment and trespassing, the wait was 42% longer in the lowest-income fifth (4.0 minutes), 28% longer in majority-Black and 40% longer in majority-Hispanic neighborhoods. For calls that were not about a crime in progress, such as possible crimes to investigate, ambulance cases, disputes and alarms, the gaps were 69%, 45% and 79% (9 to 17 minutes). All six are above the 20% bar.

Call type, hour of the week, how busy the precinct was, distance to the station house and officers assigned per call barely moved these gaps. Comparing neighborhoods inside the same precinct removed them: there, calls from the lowest-income fifth waited 8% less than calls from the highest-income fifth for Critical calls and 11% less for calls not about a crime in progress; calls from majority-Black neighborhoods waited about as long as those from majority-White neighborhoods (from -5% to -2% across the four priorities, each within the margin of error). That locates the differences between precincts, not inside them; it does not explain them. The data does not show which precinct differences matter, such as how many officers are on patrol in a given hour.

The gaps for the lowest-income fifth and majority-Hispanic neighborhoods held in 2022-23, 2024-25 and the first half of 2026, which was set aside as a check, and for non-critical crimes in progress they grew (35% in 2022-23, 66% in 2026 for the lowest-income fifth). Those for majority-Black neighborhoods were smaller in 2026: 25% for calls not about a crime in progress, against 51% in 2022-23, and 12% for non-critical crimes in progress, under the 20% bar.

Limits

This shows what, not why

The data shows differences in recorded times between groups of neighborhoods. It does not show why: how units were deployed, what else they were handling, or how dispatchers chose.

Where calls come from

Times depend on which calls are made, from where, and how call takers classify them. Neighborhoods differ in all three; holding the call type fixed does not hold fixed the judgment behind it.

How the system records time

The clock starts when the call taker enters the job, not when the phone rings, and stops when a unit is marked as arrived. Officers mark their own arrival, and some calls never get an arrival time.

Neighborhoods, not people

Groups describe neighborhoods by their residents' income and makeup (Census estimates). The data says nothing about who made a call.

Calls from the public are separated by rule

The data has no field for whether an event started with a call. 15,560,037 events were left out: directed patrols and visits (75 codes), officers asking for assistance (13 codes), ShotSpotter alerts, and events with an arrival logged in the same second as the entry or dispatch.

Calls with no arrival

Calls with no recorded arrival are left out of the waits. For calls not about a crime in progress their share is highest in both the lowest-income fifth (45%) and the highest-income fifth (42%), and in majority-Hispanic (45%) and majority-White (43%) neighborhoods, against 37% in majority-Black neighborhoods. Counting them as waiting until the event was closed widens the gaps for less urgent calls.

Staffing is a 2026 snapshot

Officers per call counts uniformed officers assigned to each precinct on 2026-10-04, after the window, not officers on patrol in a given hour. It is the only public precinct staffing figure found.

Distance is a straight line

Distance runs from the call to its precinct's station house. Patrol cars are usually out on patrol, not at the station.

A small group

Only 6 neighborhoods are majority-Asian. Their results are in the tables but not in the headline.

Precinct lines changed

The 116th Precinct was formed from part of the 105th in December 2024. Each call keeps the precinct it was coded to at the time.

Method and sources

Calls from NYPD's dispatch data on NYC Open Data, 2022 to 2025, with January to June 2026 held out as a check. Rows that repeat an event are combined, keeping the first entry, dispatch and arrival. The wait runs from the call being entered (ADD_TS) to the first arrival (ARRIVD_TS), for calls with an arrival within 24 hours; each of NYPD's four priority categories is analyzed on its own. Neighborhoods are the 197 residential 2020 Neighborhood Tabulation Areas, grouped by median household income (fifths of residents) and by the group that is more than half of residents (Hispanic of any race; Black, White and Asian non-Hispanic), from the ACS 2024 5-year estimates. Each group's calls are reweighted to the citywide mix of call type, hour of the week and precinct busyness; 95% intervals come from 2,000 resamples of neighborhoods, and p-values are Holm-adjusted across the 20 planned comparisons. The plan, with the smallest gaps that matter (1 minute for Critical calls, 20% for the rest), was committed before the analysis ran. Citywide medians match the city's 911 End-to-End figures.

NYPD Calls for Service, Historic (d6zx-ckhd) and Year to Date (n2zq-pubd); NYC 911 End-to-End Data (t7p9-n9dy); 2020 Neighborhood Tabulation Areas (9nt8-h7nd), 2020 Census Tracts (63ge-mke6) and Police Precincts (y76i-bdw7), all NYC Open Data; NYPD precinct list, geocoded with NYC Planning GeoSearch; NYPD Officer Profile (pmsy-ewrc), counts by command; American Community Survey via Census Reporter.
